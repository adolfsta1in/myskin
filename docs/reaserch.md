# Psoriasis: A Deep-Dive Report on Types, Causes, Treatments, and the 2025–2026 Landscape

Psoriasis is a lifelong, immune-driven disease that affects the whole body, not just the skin, and it cannot be cured. It can, however, be controlled: many people now reach clear or almost-clear skin, and 2026 brought the first highly effective pills. The oral IL-23 receptor blocker icotrokinra (Icotyde) was approved by the FDA on March 18, 2026. Another pill, zasocitinib, has been under FDA Priority Review since Takeda's September 14, 2026 announcement, with a decision due in Q1 2027. The practical priorities are to match treatment to severity and to the areas affected, to screen early for psoriatic arthritis and heart and metabolic risk, and to use biosimilars and newer oral drugs to overcome cost barriers.

*This report is for education only. It does not replace individual medical advice, so please see a dermatologist (and a rheumatologist if you have joint symptoms) before making treatment decisions.*

---

## TL;DR

- **What it is:** Psoriasis is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory disease. It is driven mainly by the IL-23/Th17/IL-17 immune pathway in people who are genetically susceptible (the strongest risk gene is HLA-C*06:02). Plaque psoriasis is the most common form, and about 1 in 5 patients also develops psoriatic arthritis. It is not contagious and not curable, but it is highly controllable.
- **How it's treated:** Treatment follows severity. Mild disease is treated with topicals: steroids, vitamin D analogues, and the newer non-steroidal creams roflumilast and tapinarof. Moderate-to-severe disease is treated with phototherapy, conventional pills, targeted oral drugs, or biologics. The 2025 Cochrane network meta-analysis ranks infliximab, bimekizumab, ixekizumab, and risankizumab (plus xeligekimab, which is approved in China) as the most effective drugs for reaching 90% improvement (PASI 90).
- **What's new (2025–2026):**
  - The FDA approved oral icotrokinra on March 18, 2026, for patients aged 12 and older who weigh at least 40 kg. Johnson & Johnson said the approval rested on four Phase III ICONIC studies in 2,500 patients. In head-to-head trials it roughly doubled or tripled complete skin clearance compared with deucravacitinib.
  - Deucravacitinib gained a psoriatic arthritis approval on March 6, 2026.
  - Zasocitinib beat deucravacitinib head-to-head (36.5% vs 13.9% completely clear) and has an FDA action date in Q1 2027.
  - Ustekinumab biosimilars launched in the US in 2025 at 80–92% list-price discounts. Mark Cuban Cost Plus Drug Company lists one of them, Starjemza, from $345 per quarter, a roughly 98.8% discount according to Biosimilars Rx Review.

## Key Findings

1. **The size of the burden depends on which estimate you use.** Global estimates range from about 43 million people (Global Burden of Disease, GBD 2021) to about 55.8 million adults (Global Psoriasis Atlas, Parisi et al., BMJ 2020; the University of Manchester press release rounded this to 60 million) to the 100–125 million often quoted by the WHO and patient groups. The differences come from methods and data gaps: in 2017, prevalence data existed for only 20 countries.
2. **Psoriasis is systemic.** About 19.7% of patients have psoriatic arthritis, pooled across 266 studies and 976,408 patients (Alinaghi et al., JAAD 2019). About 15.5% have arthritis that has not been diagnosed (Villani et al., JAAD 2015). Guidelines recommend cardiovascular and metabolic screening for everyone with psoriasis.
3. **Biologics remain the most effective drugs, and oral options are closing the gap fast.** In 2025 Cochrane confirmed that biologics beat both conventional and targeted oral drugs for PASI 90. But icotrokinra reaches PASI 90 in about 55–57% of patients at week 16, and zasocitinib in about 52–63%. That is well above older pills: apremilast reaches clear or almost-clear skin (sPGA 0/1) in about 30%, and deucravacitinib reaches PASI 90 in about 30–34%.
4. **Treating early may change the course of the disease.** In the GUIDE trial, patients treated with guselkumab within 2 years of onset became "super-responders" more often and stayed treatment-free longer after the drug was withdrawn (median 378 vs 259 days). This is promising but not yet proof that early treatment modifies the disease.
5. **Cost is falling for some drug classes.** Ustekinumab biosimilars now cost a fraction of the brand, as adalimumab biosimilars already did for TNF inhibitors.

## 1. Overview and Epidemiology

**What it is.** Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disease that mainly affects the skin, nails, and joints. It also raises the risk of cardiometabolic, psychiatric, and other conditions. In 2014, World Health Assembly Resolution WHA67.9 formally recognized psoriasis as a "serious noncommunicable disease." The WHO followed with its first *Global Report on Psoriasis* in 2016. The report estimated that about 100 million people are affected. It called for:
- inclusion of psoriasis care in universal health coverage;
- better access to affordable medicines;
- action against stigma.

**How common it is, and why the estimates conflict:**

| Source | Estimate | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WHO Global Report (2016) | ~100 million worldwide | Advocacy-oriented estimate |
| National Psoriasis Foundation / World Psoriasis Day | 125 million (2–3%) | Widely quoted and probably high |
| Global Psoriasis Atlas (Parisi et al., BMJ 2020) | ~55.8 million adults (rounded to 60 million in the University of Manchester press release) | Bayesian modelling; the most rigorous peer-reviewed global estimate |
| GBD 2021 analysis | 42.98 million cases in 2021 (up 86% from 23.06 million in 1990) | Growth partly reflects population growth and aging |
| WHO-commissioned systematic review (Michalek et al., 2017) | Adults 0.51%–11.43%; children 0%–1.37% | Data came from only 20 countries |

My interpretation: the Global Psoriasis Atlas figure of about 55.8 million adults is the most defensible number. The 125 million figure is useful for advocacy but should not be treated as a measurement.

**Regional variation.**
- Prevalence is highest in high-income, mostly European-ancestry populations. It is lower in East Asia and much of Africa.
- In the US, about 7.55 million adults have psoriasis. By group, prevalence is about 3.6% in White, 1.9% in African American, and 1.6% in Hispanic adults. A later analysis found 1.5% in African Americans and 2.5% in Asian Americans.
- A 2025 survey applying one method across 20 countries unexpectedly found the highest self-reported prevalence in East Asia (5.7%). It is an outlier and conflicts with earlier data.

**Central Asia and Kyrgyzstan.** Data here are thin.
- The Global Psoriasis Atlas groups Central Asia (Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Mongolia, and others) with regions whose estimates are *extrapolated* because no directly observed data exist.
- A 2024 review from Kazakhstan reported an incidence of **47.7 per 100,000 people per year in Kyrgyzstan**. That is close to, but below, the global age-standardized incidence of 57.8 per 100,000. The same review cites a modelled prevalence of **about 0.86%** for the Central Asian countries.
- Estimates for Kazakhstan range from 0.86% to 2.5%. Kazakhstan has funded biologic therapy through compulsory medical insurance since 2020.

Kyrgyzstan-specific prevalence has not been well studied. The available figures likely underestimate the true burden because of under-diagnosis and limited access to dermatologists.

**Age of onset.** Psoriasis can start at any age, but onset is bimodal:
- **Type I (early onset)** peaks at about 16–22 years. It is more often linked to HLA-C*06:02, a family history, and more severe disease.
- **Type II (late onset)** peaks at about 55–60 years.

Prevalence rises with age. Men and women are affected about equally overall.

**Impact on quality of life.** A 1999 JAAD study by Rapp, Feldman and colleagues, titled "Psoriasis causes as much disability as other major medical diseases," used the SF-36 questionnaire. It found that psoriasis impairs quality of life about as much as cancer, arthritis, hypertension, heart disease, diabetes, and depression. The WHO report notes that the burden builds up over a lifetime and can become irreversible, a concept called "cumulative life course impairment."

## 2. Pathophysiology

**The immune cascade, simplified:**
1. **Trigger.** Something starts the process: skin injury, an infection, stress, or a drug. Damaged keratinocytes (skin cells) release self-DNA and RNA bound to the antimicrobial peptide LL-37. This activates plasmacytoid dendritic cells, which release interferon-α.
2. **Dendritic cells activate.** Myeloid dendritic cells then produce **TNF-α, IL-12, and above all IL-23**.
3. **The Th17 axis switches on.** IL-23 drives the growth and survival of **Th17 cells**, Tc17 cells, γδ T cells, and group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s). These cells secrete **IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22**, and TNF-α.
4. **Skin cells overreact.** IL-17 and IL-22 make keratinocytes multiply too fast. The skin turns over in about 3–5 days instead of about 28 (**epidermal hyperproliferation**). The cells also mature abnormally: nuclei are retained in the outer layer (**parakeratosis**). The keratinocytes release chemokines, antimicrobial peptides, and IL-36, which recruit more immune cells. This creates a self-sustaining feed-forward loop.
5. **Memory.** Tissue-resident memory T cells stay in healed skin. This explains why psoriasis often returns in the same spots after treatment is stopped.

This is why the drugs that block **IL-23** or **IL-17** work so well. It also explains why **pustular psoriasis is biologically different**: it is driven mainly by **IL-36** and innate (neutrophil) inflammation, often with mutations in *IL36RN*, and it responds to IL-36 receptor blockade (spesolimab).

**Genetics:**
- **PSORS1** on chromosome 6p21, within the MHC region, is the major susceptibility locus. Its key risk allele is **HLA-C*06:02** (older name: HLA-Cw6). It raises risk roughly 10-fold, and roughly 20-fold in people who carry two copies, according to secondary estimates.
- HLA-C*06:02 is associated with early onset, guttate psoriasis, and streptococcal triggering. It is *less* associated with psoriatic arthritis. One proposed autoantigen it presents is **ADAMTSL5**, a melanocyte protein.
- More than 60 other risk loci involve the IL-23/Th17 pathway (*IL23R, IL12B, IL23A, IL17RA*), NF-κB signaling, the skin barrier, and *CARD14*. Generalized pustular psoriasis is linked to *IL36RN*, *CARD14*, and *AP1S3* variants.

**Environmental triggers:**
- **Infections:** streptococcal throat infection classically triggers guttate psoriasis. HIV can cause severe, treatment-resistant flares.
- **Drugs:** lithium, beta-blockers, antimalarials (chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine), interferons, and imiquimod. Rapid withdrawal of systemic corticosteroids can trigger erythrodermic or pustular flares. So-called "paradoxical psoriasis" can occur with TNF inhibitors, which are themselves psoriasis treatments.
- **Skin injury (Koebner phenomenon):** new lesions can appear at sites of scratches, tattoos, sunburn, or surgery.
- **Lifestyle:** smoking (strongly linked to palmoplantar pustulosis), heavy alcohol use, and obesity. Obesity is a causal risk factor and also reduces response to treatment.
- **Psychological stress:** especially often reported by Asian and Hispanic patients.
- **Weather:** cold, dry winters and low sunlight usually make psoriasis worse. Summer sun usually helps, though sunburn can trigger Koebner lesions.

## 3. Types of Psoriasis

| Type | Appearance | Typical sites | Who / how common | Severity | Treatment notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Plaque (vulgaris)** | Sharply bordered, raised, red (violaceous or gray on dark skin) plaques with silvery-white scale | Elbows, knees, scalp, lower back, belly button, gluteal cleft | About 80–90% of patients | Mild to severe | Full treatment ladder (topicals → phototherapy → systemic drugs) |
| **Guttate** | Many small (2–10 mm) drop-shaped scaly papules appearing suddenly | Trunk, upper limbs | Children and young adults; often 1–3 weeks after strep throat | Usually mild to moderate; can clear on its own, and up to about a third go on to plaque disease | Treat strep if present (evidence for antibiotics is weak); topicals; NB-UVB works especially well |
| **Inverse / flexural** | Smooth, shiny, well-defined red patches with little scale; maceration and fissures | Armpits, groin, under the breasts, genitals, skin folds | Common in people with obesity; often misdiagnosed | Out of proportion to area involved (pain, sexual impact) | Avoid potent steroids long-term (skin thinning); use calcineurin inhibitors, roflumilast (approved for folds), or low-potency steroids; rule out yeast infection |
| **Generalized pustular (GPP, von Zumbusch)** | Sudden widespread sterile pustules on red skin, with fever and malaise | Widespread | Rare; linked to *IL36RN*; triggered by steroid withdrawal, pregnancy (impetigo herpetiformis), or infection | **Potentially life-threatening** (sepsis, multi-organ failure) | Emergency care; **spesolimab** (IV for flares, subcutaneous to prevent flares); alternatives include cyclosporine, acitretin, infliximab, IL-17/IL-23 biologics (approved for GPP in Japan) |
| **Palmoplantar pustulosis (PPP)** | Sterile yellow pustules that turn brown, on red, scaly palms and soles | Palms and soles | Mostly women aged 40–60; **strongly linked to smoking** | Very disabling for walking and hand use | Notoriously hard to treat: potent steroids under occlusion, acitretin, PUVA, apremilast; biologics less consistent; stop smoking |
| **Erythrodermic** | Redness over more than about 75–90% of body surface; peeling; chills | Whole body | Rare (about 1–2%); after unstable plaque disease or abrupt steroid withdrawal | **Medical emergency** (fluid and temperature loss, infection, heart failure) | Hospitalization; cyclosporine or infliximab for rapid control; IL-17/IL-23 biologics; avoid systemic steroids |
| **Nail** | Pitting, oil-drop/salmon spots, onycholysis (nail lifting), subungual hyperkeratosis, crumbling | Fingernails and toenails | Up to about half of patients, and most patients with psoriatic arthritis | Strongly predicts psoriatic arthritis | Topicals work poorly; steroid injections into the nail area; IL-17 and IL-23 biologics are most effective (response takes 6–12 months) |
| **Scalp** | Thick adherent scale, often past the hairline; itch; temporary hair shedding | Scalp, behind the ears | About half or more of patients; more common in Asian and Black patients | High quality-of-life impact | Steroid or steroid–calcipotriol gels, foams, and shampoos; salicylic acid or coal tar to lift scale; roflumilast foam; systemic drugs if severe |
| **Palmoplantar (non-pustular)** | Thick fissured plaques on the hands and feet | Palms and soles | Common among manual workers | Disabling even at low body surface area | Counts as "special site" disease, which qualifies for systemic therapy |
| **Genital** | Red, smooth, sometimes tender patches | Genitals | Up to about 60% at some point, often not reported | Major sexual and quality-of-life impact | Low-potency steroids, calcineurin inhibitors, roflumilast; systemic drugs (e.g., icotrokinra 77% clear or almost clear vs 21% on placebo at week 16) |

**Psoriatic arthritis (PsA).** PsA is an inflammatory arthritis of the peripheral joints, spine, and entheses (where tendons attach to bone). It also causes dactylitis ("sausage digits").
- A 2019 JAAD meta-analysis (266 studies, 976,408 patients) found PsA in **19.7%** of people with psoriasis. The rate was 22.7% in Europeans, 14.0% in Asians, and 3.3% in children.
- New PsA develops at a rate of **0.27 to 2.7 cases per 100 patient-years** among people with psoriasis (Alinaghi et al., JAAD 2019).
- A 2015 JAAD meta-analysis (Villani et al.) found undiagnosed PsA in **15.5%** of patients across all studies, and in 10.1% when only epidemiological studies were counted.
- In most cases the skin disease comes first; arthritis appears first in only about 13–17%.
- Predictors include nail disease, scalp and intergluteal lesions, severe skin disease, and obesity.
- Joint damage can be permanent within the first few years, so new joint pain, morning stiffness, heel pain, or a swollen digit warrants referral to a rheumatologist.
- Treatment choice changes when PsA is present. TNF, IL-17, IL-23, and IL-12/23 inhibitors, apremilast, JAK inhibitors, methotrexate, and (since March 2026) deucravacitinib all have PsA data or approvals. Icotrokinra is **not yet** approved for PsA; its phase 3 trials are ongoing.

## 4. Symptoms and Severity Assessment

**Symptoms:** itch (up to about 80–90% of patients), burning, pain, bleeding from cracks, scaling and shedding, nail changes, joint pain, fatigue, and poor sleep.

**Scoring tools:**
- **BSA (body surface area):** the patient's own palm with fingers is about 1% of body surface.
- **PASI (Psoriasis Area and Severity Index):** scores 0–72 based on redness, thickness, and scale in four body regions, weighted by area. **PASI 75/90/100** means a 75%, 90%, or 100% improvement from the starting score. PASI 90 is now the expected target, and an absolute PASI of 2 or less is often the goal.
- **PGA / IGA (Physician's / Investigator's Global Assessment):** a 5–6-point static scale. A score of 0/1 means "clear or almost clear."
- **DLQI (Dermatology Life Quality Index):** scores 0–30. A score of 0/1 means psoriasis has no effect on the patient's life; above 10 means a very large effect.
- **Site-specific tools:** NAPSI (nails), PSSI or ss-IGA (scalp), sPGA-G (genitals), and PPPASI (palmoplantar pustulosis).

**Severity categories.** The traditional "rule of tens" in European guidelines defines moderate-to-severe disease as **BSA over 10%, PASI over 10, or DLQI over 10**. The AAD uses a similar rule of thumb: mild disease is under 3% BSA, moderate 3–10%, and severe over 10%.

Special sites (scalp, face, nails, palms and soles, genitals), resistance to topicals, or PsA can *upgrade* the severity rating and qualify someone for systemic therapy even when BSA is low. The International Psoriasis Council's approach is simpler: a patient either needs topicals only, or needs systemic therapy.

Scoring caveat for skin of color: PASI's redness component underestimates severity on darker skin, and none of these scores include post-inflammatory dyspigmentation.

## 5. Diagnosis

**Clinical diagnosis.** Most psoriasis is diagnosed by examination, based on the shape and distribution of lesions (extensor surfaces, scalp, nails, gluteal cleft), the Auspitz sign (pinpoint bleeding when scale is removed), the Koebner phenomenon, nail pitting, and family history. Dermoscopy, which shows evenly spaced dotted blood vessels, is a helpful aid.

**When a biopsy is used:** when the diagnosis is unclear, the presentation is atypical, or treatment is failing. Typical findings are a thickened epidermis with elongated rete ridges, parakeratosis, a thin or absent granular layer, Munro microabscesses (neutrophils collected in the outer layer), and dilated, twisted blood vessels in the dermal papillae.

**Supporting tests:**
- KOH scraping or fungal culture to exclude tinea (fungal infection).
- Throat swab or ASO titre for suspected guttate psoriasis.
- HIV testing for explosive or treatment-resistant disease.
- Screening before systemic therapy: complete blood count, liver and kidney function, lipids, HbA1c, hepatitis B and C, HIV, tuberculosis (IGRA), and pregnancy test.

**Differential diagnosis:** eczema (less sharply bordered, oozing, flexural, with atopy); seborrheic dermatitis (greasy yellow scale on the scalp, eyebrows, and nasal folds; overlap is called "sebopsoriasis"); tinea (annular with an active edge, KOH positive); lichen planus (purple, flat-topped papules with Wickham striae); pityriasis rosea (herald patch, mimics guttate); secondary syphilis (palms and soles; check serology); mycosis fungoides (consider in "psoriasis" that doesn't respond); and lupus, drug eruptions, candidiasis, onychomycosis, and tinea versicolor (a misdiagnosis on dark skin).

## 6. Comorbidities and Screening

| Comorbidity | Evidence summary | Screening recommendation (AAD-NPF 2019 and others) |
|---|---|---|
| **Psoriatic arthritis** | 19.7% prevalence; 15.5% undiagnosed | Ask about joint pain, stiffness, dactylitis, and enthesitis at every visit (tools include PEST and ToPAS); refer early |
| **Cardiovascular disease** | Higher rates of heart attack, stroke, atrial fibrillation, and cardiovascular death, especially in severe disease | CV risk assessment for **all** patients per national guidelines. Screen earlier and more often if the patient needs systemic therapy or phototherapy or has BSA over 10%. The AAD-NPF suggests multiplying the calculated CV risk score by **1.5** in severe psoriasis |
| **Metabolic syndrome, obesity, diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension** | Obesity both causes psoriasis and blunts treatment response; higher risk of type 2 diabetes | Blood pressure, fasting glucose and/or HbA1c, lipids, BMI, and waist circumference according to national guidelines; more frequent in severe disease |
| **Metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MASLD/NAFLD)** | Increased; relevant for methotrexate safety | Liver tests; consider fibrosis scoring (FIB-4, elastography) |
| **Inflammatory bowel disease** | Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis are increased | Ask about gut symptoms; IL-17 inhibitors are generally avoided in IBD; IL-23 and IL-12/23 drugs and TNF inhibitors treat both |
| **Depression, anxiety, suicidality** | Much higher rates | Screen (PHQ-2/9, GAD-7); caution with brodalumab (boxed warning); refer to mental health services |
| **Others** | Chronic kidney disease, COPD, obstructive sleep apnea, uveitis, celiac disease, some cancers (lymphoma, keratinocyte cancers), alcohol misuse | Assess clinically |

This matters because studies repeatedly show that cardiometabolic risk factors in psoriasis patients are **under-recognized and undertreated**. Dermatologists and primary care doctors need to share responsibility. Patients can help by asking their GP for regular blood pressure, lipid, and HbA1c checks.

## 7. Treatments

Treatment has a clear goal. **At least PASI 75** is the minimum target in European (EuroGuiDerm) guidance. **PASI 90 or an absolute PASI below 2 is the desirable goal**, along with a DLQI of 0/1. If the target isn't met after the induction phase (typically 12–16 weeks), guidelines recommend changing therapy.

### 7.1 Topical therapy (first line for mild disease; add-on for all)

- **Corticosteroids.** Potency classes run from I (super-potent, e.g., clobetasol propionate 0.05%) through VII (least potent, e.g., hydrocortisone 1%).
  - Use potent or super-potent steroids for thick plaques, the scalp, and palms and soles. Use low potency for the face, folds, and genitals.
  - Risks include skin thinning, stretch marks, tachyphylaxis (reduced effect over time), and, with overuse, adrenal suppression and rebound or pustular flares.
  - A typical approach is daily use for 2–4 weeks, then weekend-only "pulse" maintenance.
- **Vitamin D analogues (calcipotriol/calcipotriene, calcitriol).** Steroid-sparing and suitable for long-term use; can irritate the skin.
- **Fixed combinations.**
  - Calcipotriol + betamethasone dipropionate (ointment, gel, or foam) is one of the most effective topicals.
  - Halobetasol + tazarotene lotion is another option.
- **Calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus 0.1% ointment, pimecrolimus).** Used off-label for the face, folds, and genitals; they cause no skin thinning.
- **Tazarotene (topical retinoid).** Works for plaques; irritating; contraindicated in pregnancy.
- **Keratolytics and older agents:**
  - Salicylic acid (2–10%) lifts scale.
  - Coal tar has an old evidence base, stains, and smells.
  - Anthralin (dithranol) is used in short-contact regimens and stains skin and fabric.
- **Newer non-steroidal topicals:**
  - **Roflumilast 0.3% cream** (PDE4 inhibitor, once daily) is approved for plaque psoriasis including skin folds. In 2026 the FDA extended approval to **children aged 2 and older**, making it the first topical PDE4 inhibitor for plaque psoriasis in under-6s. It has no limit on duration or body area. A foam version is available for the scalp and body.
  - **Tapinarof 1% cream** (aryl hydrocarbon receptor agonist, once daily) is approved for adult plaque psoriasis. It can cause folliculitis and has been associated with fairly long remissions off treatment in trials.

### 7.2 Phototherapy

- **Narrowband UVB (NB-UVB, 311–313 nm):** the workhorse. Given 2–3 times a week for about 20–30 sessions. Safe in pregnancy and children, with minimal cancer risk. Excellent for guttate and widespread thin plaques.
- **PUVA (psoralen + UVA):** more effective for thick plaques, palmoplantar disease, and pustulosis. Carries a dose-dependent risk of squamous cell carcinoma (and possibly melanoma), so it is now used less.
- **Excimer laser (308 nm):** targeted treatment of localized, resistant plaques and the scalp.
- **Home phototherapy:** as effective as clinic treatment and more convenient (the UK PLUTO study; the US LITE trial). It is increasingly covered by insurers.

### 7.3 Conventional (non-targeted) systemic drugs

| Drug | How it's given | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **Methotrexate** | Weekly, oral or subcutaneous (typically 7.5–25 mg), with folic acid | Cheap and widely available; also helps peripheral PsA. Risks: liver fibrosis (worse with alcohol, obesity, diabetes, MASLD), bone-marrow suppression, lung toxicity, nausea. **Teratogenic**: AAD-NPF advises waiting **at least 3 months** after stopping before pregnancy, and says men should reasonably wait 3 months too. Cochrane: superior to apremilast for PASI 90 |
| **Cyclosporine (ciclosporin)** | 2.5–5 mg/kg/day in two doses | Fast, used for rescue (erythrodermic, pustular, and unstable disease). Limit to about 1 year (US) or 2 years (Europe) because of kidney toxicity, high blood pressure, and skin cancer risk |
| **Acitretin** | Oral retinoid, 10–50 mg/day | Best for pustular and palmoplantar disease and for people who are immunosuppressed or HIV-positive, because it doesn't suppress immunity. Modest effect on plaques used alone. **Severely teratogenic**: AAD-NPF advises women to wait **at least 3 years** after stopping before pregnancy (the European label says 2 years), and to avoid alcohol, which converts it to the longer-lasting etretinate |
| **Fumarates (dimethyl fumarate, Skilarence; Fumaderm)** | Oral, slowly increased dose | Common first-line choice in Germany and Europe; not available in the US. Side effects include flushing and diarrhoea. Monitor white cells (rare cases of PML, a brain infection). Cochrane ranks it among the weakest for PASI 90 |
| **Apremilast (PDE4 inhibitor)** | Oral, 30 mg twice daily after a dose ramp-up | No lab monitoring. Approved for plaque, scalp, genital, and (in the US) *mild* psoriasis, PsA, and pediatric use. Side effects: diarrhoea and nausea early on, weight loss, possible mood effects. Modest efficacy (about 30% sPGA 0/1 in recent comparator arms) |

### 7.4 Targeted oral small molecules

| Agent | Target | Status (Oct 2026) | Efficacy highlights |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deucravacitinib (Sotyktu)** | TYK2 (allosteric) | Approved for moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis (2022) and **active PsA (FDA, March 6, 2026)** | PASI 90 about 30–34% at week 16 in recent comparator arms; once daily; no boxed warning like JAK inhibitors |
| **Icotrokinra (Icotyde)** | **IL-23 receptor (oral peptide)** | **FDA approved March 18, 2026** for adults and adolescents ≥12 years (≥40 kg) with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis; also approved by the European Commission | ICONIC-LEAD: IGA 0/1 65% vs 8%, PASI 90 50% vs 4% at week 16, rising to 74% and 65% at week 24. ICONIC-ADVANCE 1/2: PASI 90 55%/57% vs 4%/1% on placebo, and vs about 30%/34% on deucravacitinib; PASI 100 31% vs 11% on deucravacitinib (ADVANCE 1). Adverse events similar to placebo (48% vs 57% to week 16). Scalp ss-IGA 0/1 66%; genital 77%. Must be taken on an empty stomach. Not yet approved for PsA |
| **Zasocitinib (TAK-279)** | TYK2 (highly selective) | **Investigational.** NDA accepted under Priority Review on Sept 14, 2026; **FDA action date expected Q1 2027**; EMA filing accepted | LATITUDE 3001/3002: sPGA 0/1 71.4%/69.2% vs placebo 10.7%/12.6% and apremilast 32.1%/29.7%; PASI 90 61.3%/51.9%; PASI 100 33.4%/25.2%. **LATITUDE Atlas vs deucravacitinib:** PASI 100 36.5% vs 13.9%; PASI 90 62.5% vs 34.0% |
| **JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, upadacitinib)** | JAK1/3, JAK1 | Approved for **PsA**, not for skin-only psoriasis in the US/EU | Boxed warnings: serious infections, blood clots, major cardiovascular events, and cancer (ORAL Surveillance). Reserved mainly for PsA |

**My interpretation.** Oral therapy has split into two tiers. Older oral options (apremilast, and to a lesser degree deucravacitinib) give moderate clearance. The newer oral agents (icotrokinra, and zasocitinib if approved) reach PASI 90 rates in the 50–60% range at week 16. That is roughly the level of ustekinumab or adalimumab, still somewhat below the best IL-17 and IL-23 biologics.

Two uncertainties remain: whether that efficacy is maintained over the long term, and how the pills compare directly with top-tier biologics. The ICONIC-ASCEND trial, comparing icotrokinra with ustekinumab, will be the first test.

### 7.5 Biologics

| Class | Drugs | Maintenance dosing (typical) | Relative efficacy (PASI 90) | Key safety points | Best suited for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Comparative efficacy evidence:**
- The **Cochrane living network meta-analysis** (Sbidian et al., update of August 6, 2025) found that, compared with placebo, the most effective drugs for **PASI 90** were **infliximab, xeligekimab, bimekizumab (high certainty), ixekizumab, and risankizumab**, with **no evidence of a difference among them**.
- The same review found that infliximab, IL-17 drugs, and risankizumab and guselkumab outperformed ustekinumab, tildrakizumab, adalimumab, certolizumab, and etanercept.
- Biologics outperformed both non-targeted and targeted oral drugs, and ciclosporin and methotrexate beat apremilast.
- Serious adverse events did not differ significantly from placebo for most drugs over the short term.
- **Caveats:**
  - NMA rankings shift with methods. Infliximab's rank ranged from 2nd down to 6th–7th across alternative analyses.
  - The rankings reflect *short-term* (week 8–24) data. Over the long term, IL-23 inhibitors often maintain response better than IL-17 or TNF inhibitors.
  - Head-to-head trials:
    - risankizumab beat secukinumab at week 52 (IMMerge);
    - bimekizumab beat secukinumab (BE RADIANT) and adalimumab;
    - guselkumab beat secukinumab at week 48 (ECLIPSE).

**Rough benchmarks (week 10–16 PASI 90 in pivotal trials, approximate):** bimekizumab about 85–91% (BE READY reported 91%); risankizumab, ixekizumab, guselkumab, and brodalumab about 70–75%; secukinumab about 60–70%; ustekinumab and adalimumab about 40–50%; etanercept about 20%. Cross-trial comparisons are imprecise, so treat these as orientation, not rankings.

**Biosimilars and cost:**
- **Adalimumab** biosimilars (from 2023 in the US, earlier in Europe) and **etanercept/infliximab** biosimilars are widely available.
- **Ustekinumab biosimilars** launched in the US through 2025: Wezlana (January, via Optum private label), Selarsdi (85% list-price discount), Pyzchiva (80%), Yesintek (about 90%, about $3,000 per 90 mg syringe), Otulfi, Steqeyma (85%), and Imuldosa (92%, about $2,332/month).
- On November 6, 2025, Mark Cuban Cost Plus Drug Company listed Hikma's Starjemza at **a starting price of $345 per quarter (plus shipping)** for the 90 mg/mL pre-filled syringe, according to PR Newswire. Trade press quoted about $360 per syringe, which Biosimilars Rx Review called a 98.8% discount to Stelara's list price.
- **Caveat:** US list-price discounts don't translate directly into patient savings, because insurer rebates, formularies, and copays determine what patients actually pay.
- Outside the US (including Central Asia), biosimilar adalimumab, infliximab, and ustekinumab, plus methotrexate and NB-UVB, are the most cost-effective route to good control.

### 7.6 Choosing a treatment

| Situation | Reasonable first choices | Avoid / caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild, localized** | Topical steroid ± calcipotriol combination; roflumilast or tapinarof for long-term steroid-free use | Long-term super-potent steroids on the face or folds |
| **Moderate-to-severe plaque** | NB-UVB, methotrexate (cost), or directly a biologic / new oral drug; guidelines no longer require failing a conventional drug first in many systems | — |
| **Wants a pill, no labs** | Icotrokinra (if accessible); deucravacitinib; apremilast | JAK inhibitors for skin-only disease |
| **Psoriatic arthritis** | IL-17, IL-23, or TNF inhibitor; methotrexate for mild peripheral disease; deucravacitinib, apremilast, JAK inhibitors | IL-23 and ustekinumab are less effective for axial (spine) disease |
| **IBD** | TNF inhibitor (adalimumab, infliximab), ustekinumab, risankizumab, guselkumab | **IL-17 inhibitors** |
| **Heart failure or multiple sclerosis history** | IL-17, IL-23 | **TNF inhibitors** |
| **Depression / suicidality history** | Most biologics | **Brodalumab**; watch mood on apremilast |

## 8. Lifestyle and Self-Care

- **Weight loss (strongest evidence).** In 2018 the National Psoriasis Foundation Medical Board reviewed 55 studies covering 4,534 patients in *JAMA Dermatology*. It **strongly recommended a hypocaloric (calorie-reduced) diet for overweight and obese patients**.
  - A meta-analysis of randomized trials found that weight-loss interventions lowered PASI by about 2.5 points on average.
  - In one diet trial, patients who lost at least 5% of their body weight improved significantly.
  - Weight loss also improves response to biologics.
  - GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide) are being studied as add-on treatment. The evidence is early.
- **Specific diets:** a Mediterranean diet has weak but consistent evidence and is a reasonable trial. A **gluten-free diet helps only people with positive celiac serology**. Ketogenic diets and intermittent fasting show benefit mainly through weight loss.
- **Smoking.** Quitting is recommended. Smoking worsens severity, is strongly linked to palmoplantar pustulosis, and adds to cardiovascular risk.
- **Alcohol.** Cut down. Alcohol worsens psoriasis, interferes with treatment, and multiplies methotrexate's liver toxicity.
- **Stress management.** Stress is a commonly reported trigger. Psychological interventions (CBT, mindfulness) have modest supporting evidence.
- **Skin care:** use bland emollients daily and short lukewarm showers; soften scale before applying active topicals; avoid scratching and skin trauma (Koebner phenomenon); keep skin folds dry.
- **Sun.** Short, regular, sunburn-free sun exposure often helps. Sunburn triggers lesions, and people with a history of PUVA or cyclosporine treatment must be especially careful about skin cancer.
- **Supplements and alternative therapies:**
  - **Fish oil (omega-3):** the NPF found little evidence to support it for psoriasis. It is harmless for most people and may help cardiovascular health.
  - **Vitamin D supplements:** randomized trials show no meaningful benefit for skin unless the person is deficient.
  - **Turmeric/curcumin:** small, low-quality studies only; oral bioavailability is poor.
  - **Dead Sea climatotherapy and balneotherapy:** often produce real short-term improvement from UV exposure, salt baths, and rest, but remissions are usually short. Treat it as a holiday-style phototherapy, not a cure.
  - **Traditional Chinese herbal medicines:** risk of liver toxicity and contamination with steroids. Avoid anything marketed as a "cure."

## 9. Prognosis and Natural Course

- **Chronic and relapsing.** Psoriasis usually persists for life, with flares and quieter periods. Spontaneous remission happens (more often in guttate disease) but can't be predicted.
- **Not curable.** Current treatments suppress the disease, and most people relapse within months of stopping, because tissue-resident memory T cells persist in healed skin.
- **Long drug-free remissions are possible in some people.**
  - In the GUIDE trial, 303 of 880 patients (34.4%) became "super-responders," meaning PASI 0 at weeks 20 and 28.
  - After guselkumab was withdrawn, super-responders with short disease duration (≤2 years) stayed treatment-free longer (median 378 vs 259 days).
  - Treatment started within 15 months of symptom onset looked best.
  - Stopping IL-23 inhibitors generally leads to slower relapse than stopping IL-17 or TNF inhibitors.
- **Life expectancy.** Severe psoriasis is associated with a shorter life expectancy (by several years in older cohorts), mainly through cardiovascular disease. Modern systemic therapy and control of risk factors should narrow this gap, but long-term proof is still accumulating.
- **Well-controlled disease is now the norm.** With current drugs, clear or almost-clear skin is a realistic goal for most people with moderate-to-severe disease. Worldwide, the biggest remaining gaps are **access and undertreatment**.

## 10. Special Populations

**Children:** about a third of patients have their first symptoms before age 20. Plaque psoriasis is most common, with more facial, flexural, and diaper-area involvement, and guttate psoriasis is common. Pediatric AAD-NPF guidance recommends screening for obesity, for cardiovascular risk factors and diabetes when history suggests risk, and attention to bullying and mental health. Therapy follows the adult ladder with age-approved agents (see 7.6). Only 3.3% of children with psoriasis have PsA.

**Pregnancy and breastfeeding:**
- Psoriasis often improves during pregnancy and flares after delivery. Generalized pustular psoriasis of pregnancy (impetigo herpetiformis) is dangerous for both mother and fetus.
- **Considered safe:** emollients, low-to-moderate potency topical steroids, NB-UVB (with folate supplementation), and cyclosporine when needed.
- **Contraindicated:** methotrexate (stop at least 3 months before trying to conceive), acitretin (stop 3 years before, per the AAD-NPF and US label), tazarotene, and PUVA.
- **Biologics — certolizumab pegol is the preferred choice because it lacks the Fc region that carries antibodies across the placenta:**
  - In the **CRIB** study (*Ann Rheum Dis* 2018), 13 of 14 infants had no measurable drug at birth. One had a minimal level (infant-to-mother plasma ratio 0.0009, or 0.09%), and no infants had measurable levels at weeks 4 and 8.
  - In **CRADLE** (*Ann Rheum Dis* 2017), the median relative infant dose through breast milk was **0.15%** of the maternal dose (well under the 10% concern threshold), and no drug was measurable in 56% of milk samples.
  - Other monoclonal antibodies cross the placenta mainly in the third trimester. Many clinicians continue them when needed, but infants exposed late in pregnancy should **avoid live vaccines (e.g., rotavirus, BCG) for about 6 months**.
  - Evidence for the newest drugs (icotrokinra, TYK2 inhibitors, bimekizumab) is limited; they should generally be avoided until more data exist.

**Elderly patients:** more comorbidities, many medications, kidney decline, and higher infection and cancer risk. Methotrexate needs dose adjustment for kidney function, and cyclosporine is often unsuitable. IL-23 and IL-17 biologics, apremilast, and NB-UVB are often well tolerated. Older adults are underrepresented in trials.

**Skin of color:**
- **Appearance:** plaques look **violaceous (purple), gray, dark brown, or reddish-brown** rather than salmon-pink. Redness may be subtle, scale can be thicker, and involvement is often more extensive.
- **Common misdiagnoses:** eczema, lichen planus, discoid lupus, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, and tinea versicolor.
- **Aftermath of healing:** lasting **hyper- or hypopigmentation** is common after lesions heal, and many patients find it more distressing than the active plaques. PASI doesn't measure it.
- **Scalp involvement** is more common in Asian and Black patients. Hair-care practices (washing frequency, formulations) should guide which vehicle is used: oils, foams, and solutions.
- **Burden:** non-White patients report a greater quality-of-life impact and often present with more severe disease, partly because of barriers to accessing care.
- **Evidence gaps:** across one review of 24 studies, only 138 Black and 728 Latino patients were enrolled. Online image resources also underrepresent darker skin.

## 11. Mental Health, Social Impact, and Stigma

- Depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation are substantially more common in psoriasis than in the general population. Rates rise with severity and with visible, genital, or itchy disease.
- The inflammation itself (IL-17, TNF) likely contributes to depression, not just the psychological burden. Effective systemic treatment often improves mood scores.
- The WHO report found that psoriasis affects employment, relationships, and sexual health. Many patients report discrimination (for example, at swimming pools, gyms, or hairdressers) based on the false belief that psoriasis is contagious. Stigma drives concealment, social withdrawal, and alcohol misuse.
- **Practical responses:** routine screening (PHQ-9, GAD-7), referral to psychology or CBT, peer support (NPF, IFPA, national patient associations), and treating to clear skin, which reliably improves DLQI.

## 12. Latest Research and Pipeline (as of October 2026)

**Oral IL-23 pathway drugs:**
- **Icotrokinra** was approved in the US (March 2026) and the EU. It is in phase 3 trials for PsA (for example, NCT06878404 in patients not previously treated with biologics; primary completion May 2026) and in trials for ulcerative colitis.
- ICONIC-ASCEND is a head-to-head trial against ustekinumab. It is the first attempt to show a pill beating an injectable biologic.
- Its short half-life makes it easy to stop, which may appeal for pregnancy planning or around surgery. This is a hypothesis, not established practice.

**Next-generation TYK2 inhibitors:** **Zasocitinib** has an FDA decision expected in Q1 2027. It was superior to deucravacitinib in LATITUDE Atlas.

**Oral IL-17 inhibitors:** several small-molecule oral IL-17A antagonists (including DC-806/DC-853 from DICE, now owned by Eli Lilly) are in phase 2–3 development. **Status caveat:** I could not verify 2026 phase 3 readouts for these, so treat them as investigational.

**New and adjusted biologics:** **Sonelokimab** (IL-17A/F nanobody) is in development, mainly for PsA and hidradenitis. **Xeligekimab** (IL-17A) is approved in China and ranked among the top drugs in Cochrane 2025. GUIDE showed that q16w guselkumab dosing was non-inferior to q8w in super-responders.

**Disease modification and early intervention:** GUIDE and similar trials suggest that treating early may reduce the memory T cells that sustain the disease and allow longer drug-free remissions. Trials are also testing whether early biologic treatment can **prevent PsA** in high-risk patients. All of this is promising but unproven. Don't choose a drug on the promise of cure.

**Microbiome and precision medicine:** a small 2020 Central Asian study found raised gut IL-1α and lower microbial diversity in psoriasis; probiotic and fecal-transplant trials are small and preliminary. HLA-C*06:02 status may predict a better response to ustekinumab, but it is not yet used routinely.

## 13. Common Myths and Misconceptions

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| "Psoriasis is contagious." | **False.** It is immune-mediated and cannot be spread by touch, swimming pools, or sharing towels. |
| "It's just a skin problem / cosmetic." | It is a systemic inflammatory disease with arthritis, cardiovascular, metabolic, and psychiatric comorbidities. The WHO calls it a serious noncommunicable disease. |
| "Diet can cure psoriasis." | No diet cures it. Weight loss in overweight people genuinely helps, and gluten-free diets help only people with celiac disease. |
| "Biologics are too dangerous." | Modern IL-23 and IL-17 inhibitors have short-term serious adverse event rates similar to placebo in Cochrane analyses. The main risks (infection, TB reactivation, candidiasis) are screened for and manageable. |
| "Once it clears, you're cured." | It usually returns without maintenance treatment. Long remissions are possible for some but can't be guaranteed. |
| "It only affects White people." | It affects all ethnic groups. It is under-recognized in darker skin because it looks different there. |

## Recommendations (for someone living with psoriasis)

1. **Get a confirmed diagnosis and a baseline severity score** (BSA, PASI, DLQI), and ask about special-site involvement, which can qualify you for systemic therapy.
2. **Treat to a target:** clear or almost-clear skin (PASI 90 / IGA 0/1) and a DLQI of 0–1. If you aren't there after 12–16 weeks, ask to escalate or switch.
3. **Report joint symptoms early:** morning stiffness, swollen fingers or toes, and heel or back pain.
4. **Get yearly heart and metabolic checks** (blood pressure, lipids, HbA1c, weight, liver) through your GP.
5. **If you are overweight, weight loss is the single most evidence-based lifestyle step,** followed by stopping smoking and cutting down alcohol.
6. **Ask about cost-saving options:** biosimilars, methotrexate, home NB-UVB, and patient-assistance programs.
7. **Plan pregnancies with your dermatologist:** stop methotrexate at least 3 months and acitretin at least 3 years beforehand; certolizumab is the best-studied biologic in pregnancy.
8. **Look after your mental health:** ask for screening and support.

## Caveats and Uncertainties

- **Prevalence** estimates vary by a factor of 2–3 (43 million to 125 million worldwide). Data for Central Asia, including Kyrgyzstan, are sparse and partly extrapolated.
- **Comparative efficacy** comes mostly from short-term trials and network meta-analyses whose rankings change with methods.
- **The newest drugs** (icotrokinra since March 2026; zasocitinib still investigational) have limited long-term safety data. Much of the headline efficacy comes from manufacturer press releases and industry-funded trials, although key icotrokinra results are peer-reviewed in *The Lancet*.
- **Early intervention and disease modification** is a hypothesis supported mainly by one industry-sponsored trial (GUIDE).
- **Biosimilar prices** quoted are US list or cash prices; net prices and prices outside the US differ.
- **Pregnancy data** are strongest for certolizumab. For most other agents they come from registries rather than trials.
- Drug approvals, indications, and age limits differ between the FDA, the EMA, and national regulators (including in Central Asia). Check local labelling.
